Parents' Paperwork: Case Study

How I designed a shared app for adult children to run their retired parents' yearly paperwork in India.

Overview

Parents' Paperwork is a mobile app that helps adult children, often living in another city or abroad, run their retired parents' yearly paperwork in India. It turns pension, tax, bank, insurance, property and benefit rules into one shared family calendar with step-by-step guides.
Role Product designer and founder: research, product strategy, interaction design, prototyping
Timeline Concept and clickable prototype, October 2026
Platform Mobile-first app (prototype built as an interactive HTML page)
Deliverables Market and rules research, product spec, clickable prototype, system design document
Status Pre-validation: prototype ready for a WhatsApp pilot with 30 to 50 families

The problem

Retired parents in India face a fixed yearly cycle of deadlines, and missing one costs real money. Children usually find out after the damage is done.

  • Pension stops if a form is missed. Every pensioner must submit a life certificate by 30 November. Pensioners aged 80+ can submit from 1 October, everyone else from 1 November. If it is missed, pension is paused from December until it is submitted.
  • Tax is deducted that did not need to be. A declaration must reach every bank holding deposits at the start of each tax year, or TDS is cut from the interest.
  • Deadlines are spread across many institutions. Deposit maturities at several banks, insurance renewals, property tax, re-KYC, return filing, licence and passport renewals all run on different clocks.
  • Benefits go unclaimed. Seniors qualify for health cover, extra pension at 80, tax relief and concessions, but nobody tracks which ones the family is actually using.
  • Emergencies expose the gaps. In a hospital, nobody knows where the policy, the doctor's number or the will is.

For children living away, and especially NRIs, the burden is coordination: knowing what is due, who among the siblings is handling it, and how to guide a parent through a phone-based process from thousands of kilometres away.

Research and landscape

No existing product is software-first, affordable, shared across siblings and covers the full India-specific calendar. That gap is the opportunity.

Rules move, which is both the risk and the moat. During research, two rules turned out to have changed recently:

  1. From 1 April 2026, Form 121 replaced Forms 15G and 15H under the Income-tax Act, 2025. A checklist written a year earlier would already be wrong.
  2. A pensioner aged 75+ whose only income is pension and interest from the same bank can skip filing a return by giving that bank a declaration. Few families know this applies to their parent.

A product that keeps these rules current, and applies them to each family's facts, delivers value that a static checklist or a generic reminder app cannot.

Users and jobs to be done

The buyer is the adult child; the parent is a participant, not the main user. Design decisions follow from that split.

User Context Job to be done
Priya, 38, NRI in Toronto Lead coordinator; her parents are 9.5 to 10.5 hours ahead Know what is due before it is urgent, and guide Dad through it on a video call
Arjun, 41, in Bengaluru Can reach Pune in a day; handles in-person tasks See what is his, without a long WhatsApp thread
Ramesh, 81, Dad Central govt pensioner, Android phone, comfortable with calls Complete the face-scan life certificate without travelling to a bank
Sunita, 76, Mom State govt pensioner, deposits at two institutions Keep her pension, deposits and policies in order without being overwhelmed

The core job, in the family's words: "Tell us what is due, who is doing it, and exactly how to do it, before anything costs us money."

Design principles

  1. Deadlines first, documents second. People open the app because something is due. The home screen leads with the next deadline and the walkthrough to clear it.
  2. Every task explains why. Each deadline states the consequence of missing it in one or two sentences, so families can prioritise without research.
  3. Built for a shared family. Every task has one owner. Unowned tasks are flagged in orange, because "someone will do it" is how deadlines slip.
  4. Parents stay in control. The app never stores passwords or OTPs and never logs in as a parent. Parents enter their own OTPs on their own phones.
  5. Inform, never advise. The app explains what a deposit maturity or a scheme means, but never recommends where to invest. This keeps it clear of financial-advice regulation.
  6. Rules carry a date. Every rule is shown as current to a date, with the agency that owns it, because rules change.

Business model

The plan is a low-cost family subscription, priced far below human concierge services.

Stream Detail
Free tier Basic reminders for one parent
Family plan Rs 149 to 299 a month, or a discounted annual plan: both parents, siblings, documents, trackers, benefits
Referral fees From concierge partners, insurers and tax filers, only when the family chooses a hand-off
Later: B2B Sold to banks or employers as an employee benefit for staff with ageing parents

At Rs 299 a month the plan costs about 2 to 5% of a human concierge service at Rs 6,000 to 18,000 a month.

Validation plan and success metrics

Before building the app, run a two-month WhatsApp pilot with 30 to 50 families, timed to cover the November life-certificate window and the April tax declarations.

  1. Collect each family's details through a form.
  2. Build each family's calendar by hand, using the same rules the app will automate.
  3. Send reminders and step-by-step guides on WhatsApp; show the prototype to the first families for feedback.
  4. Build the app only if families stay and are willing to pay.
Metric Target Why
Life certificates submitted on time 100% of pilot pensioners The core promise
Families still active after 2 months 70% or more Shows the need recurs
Families willing to pay Rs 149+ a month 30% or more Business viability
Tasks with an owner 90% or more Tests the sibling-sharing idea
Benefits newly claimed or corrected At least 1 per 5 families Tests the benefits tracker's value

Risks, learnings and next steps

Risks

  • Trust and privacy. Families share sensitive financial data, covered by India's DPDP Act. Mitigation: no passwords or OTPs, encryption, consent per family member.
  • Rules go stale. Mitigation: every rule has an owner, a source and a review date.
  • Regulation. Mitigation: no investment advice, no handling of money.
  • Banks improve their own reminders. Mitigation: the value is across banks, insurers and government, which no single institution covers.

What I learned

  • Research changed the product: finding the Form 121 change turned "rules engine" from a feature into the core moat.
  • Showing what a family does not qualify for builds as much trust as showing what they do.
  • The parent is a participant, not the user. Designing for a guided video call shaped the walkthroughs.

Next steps

  • Verify every rule in the prototype against official sources
  • Recruit 30 to 50 pilot families before 1 November
  • Start state benefit rules with Maharashtra only
  • Usability-test the life-certificate walkthrough with three parent and child pairs
