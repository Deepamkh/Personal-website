Overview

I took Wayfare from a one-line idea to a working mobile web app with a NASA imagery globe, attraction pages, real weather and a day-by-day itinerary planner. The whole prototype is a single self-contained HTML file that runs in two modes: with Claude for AI-written details and itineraries, and on any website with live open data.

The problem

Planning a trip means juggling five to ten apps: maps for location, review sites for opinions, a weather app, booking sites, blogs for tips and a notes app for the plan itself. Each answers one question, so the traveler does the joining up.

The pain shows up at three moments:

  • Choosing where to go: it is hard to see what is near a city without scrolling long, unranked lists.
  • Deciding about one place: history, tickets, reviews, food, parking and weather live on different sites.
  • Building the plan: turning a pile of saved places into a realistic day-by-day schedule is slow and manual.

The brief: one app that covers all three moments, starting from a globe the traveler can explore.

Users and goals

The primary user is a leisure traveler planning a city trip on their phone, a few weeks to a few months ahead.

User needWhat Wayfare does
See what is worth visiting near a placeRadius search (1 to 50 km) with attractions plotted on the globe
Judge one attraction quicklyOne page with history, tickets, review themes, food, parking, tips, photo spots and weather
Know what to expect from the weather7 to 16 day outlook plus four years of monthly history
Get a realistic plan without the busyworkAn AI agent that asks for the essentials, then writes and refines an itinerary

Design goals: mobile first, one tap from map to detail, honest about where data comes from, and useful even when the AI is unavailable.

Process

The prototype went through eight iterations, each driven by testing the previous version and naming what felt wrong.

  1. Desktop prototype. A globe on the left and a panel on the right, with search, radius discovery, attraction details, weather and the itinerary agent all working end to end.
  2. Mobile app shell. Feedback: it needed to be a phone app. I rebuilt it around a full-screen globe, a draggable bottom sheet and a bottom tab bar.
  3. Attraction tab fix. The tab was locked until a place was picked, and its data calls could hit rate limits. I made it always open, with nearby and popular picks, and queued the AI calls two at a time.
  4. Realistic Earth. The flat globe looked generic. I wrote a WebGL renderer with a painted texture: oceans by depth, deserts, forests, ice, clouds, sun shading and an atmosphere.
  5. NASA imagery. I replaced the painted texture with NASA Blue Marble (day) and Black Marble (night) images, blended along the day and night line, with an optional live sun mode.
  6. Visual identity. A pastel green and black theme across light and dark modes, with pastel green markers on the globe.
  7. Portfolio build. The app, case study and system design packed into one HTML file, with the app running inside a phone frame. A black-screen bug in strict previews led me to load the app inline and bundle its map library.
  8. Live open data. On a normal website the app could only show a Paris sample. I added a live mode that pulls attractions, photos, food, parking, tips and real weather from open-data services, plus a built-in itinerary planner.

Constraints and trade-offs

Inside Claude, the prototype runs in a locked-down environment: no outside images, no third-party APIs, and AI calls limited to a couple at a time. On a normal website the opposite holds: open data is reachable but Claude is not. Each constraint forced a visible, honest compromise rather than a fake.

ConstraintTrade-off I made
No live Google, Yelp or social media dataClaude compiles details, review themes, restaurants and tips, labelled as AI-compiled with links to the real sources
No live weather APIForecast and history are simulated from climate normals and marked "Simulated"
No remote imagesAttraction headers use generated illustrations; real photos are reached through links
No satellite map tilesNASA day and night images embedded in the page (about 1.1 MB), blurring only at street level
AI rate limitsRequests queued two at a time, overview first; stale requests dropped when the user moves on
No Claude on a normal websiteAutomatic switch to live open data: OpenStreetMap, Wikipedia, Wikidata, Wikimedia Commons, Wikivoyage and Open-Meteo, plus a built-in planner
No free source for reviewsThe Reviews tab links to Google, Tripadvisor and Yelp instead of copying or rewriting reviews

The biggest lesson: showing where data comes from mattered more than making every section look complete.

Market research and positioning

Most of Wayfare's individual features already exist; the combination around a globe and an attraction-level "should I go" page does not. Research as of October 2026.

ProductStrengthGap Wayfare targets
Google Maps and AI Mode trip planningOwns places, reviews and photos; now builds itinerariesGeneral purpose; no radius discovery or multi-year weather for a visit
MindtripAll-in-one AI planning and bookingChat first, not map first
WanderlogHands-on, map-based planningManual; little help judging a single attraction
Layla (acquired by Expedia, July 2026)AI planning tied to bookingBuilt to sell bookings, not to help decide

Positioning: a map-first "decide and plan" companion. The strongest niche from research is travelers who need comfort and access information, such as families with elderly parents or pilgrims: toilets, walking distance from parking, rest points and ticket release dates.

Outcomes and next steps

The result is a working mobile prototype that covers discovery, decision and planning in one flow, for any city in the world, in a single HTML file. It has not yet been tested with users, so the next step is evidence, not more features.

What I learned

  • Constraints sharpen a prototype: every limit became a design decision the user can see.
  • Honest labels ("AI-compiled", "Simulated") are a feature, not a disclaimer.
  • Visual realism changes perception: the NASA globe made the same features feel like a real product.
  • Bundling features is not a moat; focus on one audience is.
  • Designing for two runtimes (with AI and with open data) made every feature define its data source explicitly.

Next steps

  • Test with 5 to 8 travelers planning a real trip; measure time to a finished itinerary.
  • Pick one audience (families with elderly parents, or pilgrims) and add comfort and access details.
  • Replace simulated weather with a live forecast and history service. Done for the web version (Open-Meteo).
  • Add real attraction photos from an openly licensed source. Done for the web version (Wikimedia Commons).
  • Build the production app described in the system design.
